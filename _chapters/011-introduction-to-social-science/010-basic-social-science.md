**Social science** is the disciplined, systematic study of human behavior, institutional structures, and collective societal organization. While the natural sciences examine the physical, chemical, and biological laws governing the material universe, social science investigates the emergent properties of human interaction, cultural systems, political authority, and economic distribution.

### **The Scope and Epistemology of Social Sciences**

Social science operates at the intersection of two major epistemological traditions:
1. **Positivism**: Spearheaded by Auguste Comte and Émile Durkheim, positivism asserts that the social world can be studied using the empirical, quantitative methods of the natural sciences. It seeks to uncover invariant causal laws through direct observation, hypothesis testing, and statistical measurement.
2. **Interpretivism (*Verstehen*)**: Championed by Max Weber, interpretivism argues that because human actions are imbued with subjective meanings, intentions, and values, social inquiry must focus on empathetic understanding and contextual interpretation rather than mere mechanical causation.

Empirical inquiry requires rigorous methodological grounding, drawing observable evidence from systematically gathered data rather than unexamined metaphysical speculation.

### **The Focus and Historical Origins of Social Science**

The systematic study of society emerged in the 19th century in response to the profound dislocations of the Industrial Revolution, the rise of democratic nation-states, and urbanization:
- **Auguste Comte (1798–1857)**: Coined the term *sociology* and proposed the Law of Three Stages (Theological, Metaphysical, and Positive).
- **Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)**: Established sociology as a formal academic discipline in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), emphasizing 'social facts'—patterns of behavior, values, and norms external to the individual that exercise coercive control.
- **Max Weber (1864–1920)**: Introduced 'ideal types', analyzed bureaucratic rationalization, and demonstrated the link between religious ethics and economic systems (*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*).
- **Karl Marx (1818–1883)**: Formulated historical materialism, demonstrating how the economic mode of production structures the legal and political superstructure.

### **Scientific Methods and Methodological Rigor**

Rigorous inquiry in social science follows a structured research cycle:
1. Formulation of the research puzzle and theoretical conceptualization.
2. Systematic review of existing scholarly literature.
3. Derivation of falsifiable hypotheses or exploratory research questions.
4. Operationalization of variables and selection of research design (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods).
5. Empirical data collection (surveys, archival documents, ethnography, comparative case analysis).
6. Data analysis, causal inference, and reflexive evaluation of scope conditions.

### **Three Major Sociological Paradigms**

Modern social theory is broadly organized around three foundational theoretical paradigms:
1. **Structural Functionalism (Talcott Parsons, Robert K. Merton)**: Conceives society as a complex organism whose differentiated institutions (family, state, economy, education) function in equilibrium to maintain societal stability and normative consensus.
2. **Conflict Theory (Karl Marx, C. Wright Mills)**: Rejects the equilibrium model, viewing society as divided by structural inequalities where dominant groups maintain power through coercion and ideological hegemony. Social change is driven by systemic contestation.
3. **Symbolic Interactionism (George Herbert Mead, Herbert Blumer)**: Operates at the micro-level of analysis, examining how individuals construct meaning, social identities, and shared realities through linguistic symbols, gestures, and ongoing communicative interactions.

Understanding these foundational epistemologies is essential for international relations, as paradigms like Realism, Liberalism, and Constructivism directly reflect structural conflict, institutional functionalism, and symbolic intersubjectivity on the global stage.
