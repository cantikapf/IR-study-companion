In international relations and the broader social sciences, **history** functions not merely as a chronological repository of past occurrences, but as an empirical and ontological laboratory for understanding human agency, structural continuity, and systemic transformation.

### Historiographical Traditions and the Nature of Historical Facts

The relationship between historical facts and social theory was famously formulated by British historian E.H. Carr in *What Is History?* (1961). Carr demonstrated that history is never an objective collection of neutral facts existing independently of the historian; rather, it is a dynamic, continuous process of interaction between the historian in the present and the facts of the past. Facts become meaningful only when selected and interpreted through theoretical conceptualizations.

Historiographical inquiry generally navigates two major levels of explanation:
1. **Idiographic Approaches**: Emphasize unique events, particular human contingencies, and specific biographies (e.g., diplomatic telegrams during the July Crisis of 1914).
2. **Nomothetic Approaches**: Characteristic of social science, seek to identify recurring structural patterns, systemic trends, and generalizable causal mechanisms across temporal periods.

### Structure, Agency, and the *Longue Durée*

To prevent superficial, short-term explanations of geopolitical crises, the French *Annales* School—spearheaded by Fernand Braudel—introduced a three-tiered model of historical time:
- ***Événements* (Short-Term Events)**: The rapid, surface ripples of daily political drama, diplomatic encounters, and individual decisions.
- ***Conjonctures* (Medium-Term Cycles)**: Economic fluctuations, trade cycles, demographic shifts, and regional institutional developments spanning decades.
- ***La Longue Durée* (Long-Term Structures)**: The deep, glacial transformations of geography, climate, maritime transit corridors, and foundational civilization structures that constrain state behavior across centuries.

### Historical Sociology in International Relations

In contemporary international relations, the subfield of historical sociology (exemplified by Theda Skocpol, Charles Tilly, and Michael Mann) demonstrates that contemporary international orders cannot be comprehended in a static, ahistorical vacuum. As Charles Tilly famously synthesized, *"War made the state, and the state made war."*

By analyzing how fiscal extraction, military competition, and domestic revolutions co-evolved over half a millennium, historical social science equips analysts with the critical tools necessary to deconstruct contemporary global crises, evaluate the rise and decline of great powers, and assess the enduring legacy of imperialism.
