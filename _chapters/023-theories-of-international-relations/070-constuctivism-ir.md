## Introduction to Constructivist Theory

Constructivism emerged in the late 1980s and 1990s as a powerful challenge to the dominant rationalist paradigms of Neorealism and Neoliberal Institutionalism. While rationalists assume that states possess fixed, exogenous interests (material power or economic wealth) and treat structural anarchy as an immutable constraint, constructivists demonstrate that the international system is a **socially constructed reality**.

Pioneered by **Nicholas Onuf** in *World of Our Making* (1989)—who coined the term 'constructivism'—and systematically formalized by **Alexander Wendt** in *Social Theory of International Politics* (1999), constructivism argues that material capabilities only acquire meaning through shared ideas, social norms, and intersubjective knowledge. As Wendt famously asserted in his seminal 1992 article:

> *"Anarchy is what states make of it."*

For example, 500 British nuclear warheads do not threaten the United States, whereas 5 North Korean nuclear warheads trigger grave national security alarms. Materially, Britain possesses infinitely more destructive firepower; ideationally, however, the shared democratic identity and history of alliance between London and Washington renders British weapons non-threatening.

## Alexander Wendt's Three Cultures of Anarchy

Rejecting Waltz's claim that anarchy has a single, fixed logic of self-help, Alexander Wendt (1999) demonstrated that the meaning of anarchy depends on the intersubjective role structures that states enact through continuous interaction. Wendt classified international systems into three distinct **Cultures of Anarchy**:

1. **Hobbesian Culture (Enemies)**: States view one another as existential enemies (*hostes*). The right of the other to exist is denied; violence has no normative limits, and survival requires military elimination or conquest. This logic dominated world politics in antiquity and during total war eras.
2. **Lockean Culture (Rivals)**: States view one another as rivals (*aemuli*). While states compete fiercely and may use force to advance interests, they mutually recognize each other's sovereign right to exist. Sovereignty functions as a shared regulatory norm that limits predation. This has been the dominant culture of the modern Westphalian state system since 1648.
3. **Kantian Culture (Friends)**: States view one another as friends (*amici*). Security is internationalized: states resolve disputes strictly without the threat or use of force, and an attack on one is treated as an attack on all (collective security and pluralistic security communities, such as NATO and the European Union).

Because identities and cultures of anarchy are socially reproduced through state practice, a Hobbesian system can evolve into a Lockean or Kantian system through socialization, institutionalization, and normative change.

## Agents and Structures: Mutual Constitution

A central methodological contribution of constructivism (drawing on Anthony Giddens' structuration theory) is the concept of **co-constitution** or **mutual constitution**:
- **Structures Constitute Agents**: International normative structures (sovereignty, diplomatic immunity, human rights treaties) do not merely regulate pre-existing state behavior; they **constitute** the very identity and legal personhood of states.
- **Agents Constitute Structures**: Structures have no material existence separate from human practice. Sovereign norms exist only because state leaders continuously invoke, reproduce, and respect them in daily diplomatic interactions. When actors contest existing norms and establish new practices (norm entrepreneurship), structures change.

## Structures and Agents

Constructivism emphasizes that structures not only constrain actors, they also constitute identities. The individual or state can also influence their environment as well as being influenced by it, through a process of interaction and mutual constitution. 

Structures like states, alliances or international institutions are not thought to exist independent of human meaning and action. Rather, these structures shape the identities and possibilities available to the agents that inhabit them. For example, the structure of the United Nations constitutes the identity of a country as a member state, which confers certain rights and responsibilities. 

At the same time, agents like states and individuals are not completely determined by structure. They have agency to influence and change the environment that shapes them through a process of mutual interaction. States can cooperate to reform international institutions, or an individual can advocate for policy changes in their country. Both structure and agency are important for understanding how the social world is constructed.

The emphasis on mutual constitution of structures and agents moves away from traditional IR theories that treat structures as static constraints and focuses instead on the complex interplay between the two. This dialectic relationship is key to the constructivist view that international relations are not fixed, but are socially constructed and thus contain possibility for change driven by social interaction.

## Case Study : NATO Enlargement

In his influential study *The EU, NATO and the Integration of Europe* (2001), **Frank Schimmelfennig** provided a definitive constructivist explanation for Eastern enlargement that rationalist theories could not resolve. From a neorealist perspective, expanding NATO to Russia's borders provoked unnecessary balancing without adding significant military capabilities; from a neoliberal perspective, admitting post-communist states created substantial financial and administrative burdens.

Schimmelfennig demonstrated that NATO's expansion was driven by **rhetorical action** and **rhetorical entrapment**:
1. **Pan-European Identity & Normative Standard**: NATO defines itself not merely as a military alliance, but as a community of liberal democracies bound by the Washington Treaty's core values.
2. **The "Return to Europe" Strategy**: Central and Eastern European states (starting with Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic) framed their accession as a rightful "return to Europe," shaming Western capitals to honor their stated liberal values.
3. **Rhetorical Entrapment**: Even though key Western European leaders were privately skeptical of rapid enlargement due to costs and Russian sensitivities, they could not publicly veto applicant states that met democratic standards without violating NATO's constitutive liberal identity. Rejecting them would have exposed Western powers as hypocrites, damaging their normative legitimacy.

Thus, international socialization and the politics of legitimacy—rather than material utility alone—explain why NATO expanded into post-communist Europe.
