## Introduction to Two-Level Games

In his landmark article *"Diplomacy and Domestic Politics: The Logic of Two-Level Games"* (1988), **Robert D. Putnam** resolved the longstanding dichotomy between international systemic pressures and domestic politics. Realists traditionally argue that foreign policy is determined strictly by external anarchy, while domestic-politics theorists argue that foreign policy reflects internal coalitions. Putnam demonstrated that international negotiations require state leaders to play a simultaneous game across two distinct tables:

1. **Level I (International Bargaining)**: Bargaining between state representatives or chief negotiators seeking to construct an acceptable international agreement.
2. **Level II (Domestic Ratification)**: Separate discussions within each nation's domestic constituency (legislative bodies, political parties, interest groups, bureaucratic agencies, and the public) regarding whether to formally ratify or politically accept the Level I agreement.

The central analytical insight of the two-level game is that moves which are rational for a leader at Level I may be politically suicidal at Level II, and vice versa. Chief negotiators cannot simply impose their preferred foreign policy; they are structurally constrained by domestic ratification requirements.

## The Dynamics of the Win-Set

The core concept in Putnam's model is the **Win-Set**, defined as:

> The set of all possible Level I international agreements that would gain the necessary majority support among Level II domestic constituents to achieve ratification.

The size and configuration of each negotiating party's win-set determines two critical outcomes:
1. **Likelihood of Agreement**: International agreement is possible if and only if the Level II win-sets of all negotiating states **overlap**. If win-sets do not intersect, negotiations inevitably collapse into deadlock or failed ratification.
2. **Distribution of Joint Gains (Bargaining Power)**: The relative size of the win-sets determines bargaining leverage. Paradoxically, having a **smaller win-set** can strengthen a negotiator's hand at Level I (the *"tied hands"* bargaining advantage, drawing on Thomas Schelling). A negotiator can credibly demand concessions from foreign counterparts by demonstrating: *"I would personally accept your proposal, but my domestic parliament will reject it unless you sweeten the terms."* Conversely, a large domestic win-set makes a negotiator vulnerable to pressure, as foreign counterparts know domestic ratification is easily attained.

## Determinants of Win-Set Size

The size of a state's Level II win-set is shaped by three structural determinants:

1. **Distribution of Domestic Power, Preferences, and Coalitions**: The homogeneity or heterogeneity of domestic interests. When domestic costs of an agreement are concentrated and benefits are diffuse (or vice versa), protectionist coalitions mobilize to veto ratification.
2. **Domestic Political Institutions**: Constitutional voting rules decisively alter win-set dimensions. For example, the United States Constitution requires a two-thirds supermajority in the Senate to ratify treaties (Article II, Section 2), creating a significantly narrower win-set than parliamentary systems where the executive commands a disciplined legislative majority.
3. **Level I Negotiator Strategies and Framing**: State leaders can strategically use side-payments, issue-linkage, and rhetorical persuasion to reshape domestic coalitions and expand the win-set.

## Ratification Failure: Voluntary vs. Involuntary Defection

When an international agreement fails to be implemented, Putnam distinguishes between two fundamentally different types of defection:

- **Voluntary Defection**: Occurs when a rational egoist state leader deliberately reneges on an international commitment because compliance is deemed no longer advantageous in the absence of an overarching enforcement mechanism (e.g., standard prisoner's dilemma defection).
- **Involuntary Defection**: Occurs when a state leader genuinely intends to honor a Level I international agreement but is unable to deliver on the promise because domestic Level II actors veto or refuse to ratify the accord (e.g., President Woodrow Wilson negotiating the League of Nations Covenant in Paris, only to have the US Senate reject ratification in 1919–1920; or the US signature on the Kyoto Protocol in 1997 without Senate ratification).

Recognizing involuntary defection is critical for diplomatic strategy: what appears as duplicitous bad faith from the outside is often the structural consequence of domestic political gridlock.
