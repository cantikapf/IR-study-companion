Historical Analysis: 1914

In 1914, the Triple Entente (Britain + France + Russia = 42) faced the Triple Alliance (Germany + Austria-Hungary + Italy = 34). The 10.5% imbalance might suggest stability, but several factors destabilized the balance:

  1. Chain-Ganging (Christensen & Snyder): Rigid alliance commitments meant a local Balkan crisis automatically activated the entire alliance system.
  2. Cult of the Offensive (Van Evera): Military planners on all sides believed offense had the advantage, creating pressure for preemptive mobilization.
  3. Italy's Defection: Italy actually left the Triple Alliance in 1914, further destabilizing the system.

The lesson: raw power calculations alone don't predict war. Perceptions, alliance credibility, and military doctrine matter just as much.

Theory Analysis: Balance of Power

According to neorealist scholars like Kenneth Waltz, stability in the international system depends on the distribution of power (polarity). Bipolar systems (two balanced blocs) are historically more stable than multipolar systems because miscalculation is less likely and powers balance internally rather than through complex chain-ganging. Classical realists like Hans Morgenthau argue that statesmen must actively craft balances through alliances to prevent hegemony.
